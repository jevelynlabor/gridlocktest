How to Make CSS Gradients That Actually Look Good

Gridlock Holmes team 15 October 2026 11 min read

Your CSS works. The gradient appears. Technically, you've succeeded.

So why does it still look a bit... wrong?

Maybe the middle looks muddy. Maybe one colour takes over the whole background. Maybe the transition feels harsh. Or maybe you've spent ten minutes changing the angle from 45deg to 47deg and you're no longer sure whether anything looks better.

The good news is that you usually don't need more complicated CSS. You need to make a few better decisions about the colours, the transition and the space each colour gets.

Small changes to direction and colour stops can change the feel of the same basic gradient.

1. Your colours are fighting each other

The first instinct when a gradient looks wrong is often to start changing percentages. But sometimes the problem starts before the colour stops. It starts with the two colours themselves.

Put two colours next to each other and they can look great. Blend them together and the transition between them may feel dull, harsh or unexpectedly grey.

Cause

A gradient doesn't only show your starting and ending colour. It has to create all the colours between them, and that middle part matters just as much as the two ends.

Fix

Don't judge a gradient from two swatches - judge the transition. Try bringing the colours closer together, adjusting lightness, reducing saturation, or adding a carefully chosen intermediate colour.

2. Your colour stops are doing more than you think

Start with:

background: linear-gradient(
  90deg,
  #6c3cff,
  #36d1dc
);

With no explicit stop positions, the browser distributes the colours across the gradient for you. That may be exactly what you want. But sometimes you want one colour to dominate more of the background. That's when the stop positions become useful.

background: linear-gradient(
  90deg,
  #6c3cff 0%,
  #36d1dc 70%
);

The transition now behaves differently because the second colour is reaching its full value later in the gradient.

Cause

You're changing colours but leaving the browser to decide where the transition should happen.

Fix

Move the colour stops intentionally. Ask "where do I want this colour to become dominant?" rather than "what percentage is correct?"

Stop position comparison
Even - 0% → 100%
Earlier - 0% → 65%
Later - 0% → 85%

3. Sometimes two colours are not enough

A two-colour gradient is often all you need. But if the middle of the transition is the part you dislike, adding a third colour can give you more control over the path between the two ends.

background: linear-gradient(
  90deg,
  #6c3cff 0%,
  #4f7cff 50%,
  #36d1dc 100%
);

Now the browser is not making the entire journey from purple to cyan in one step. You've given it a useful point in the middle.

Cause

The browser is interpolating directly between two colours that don't create the transition you wanted.

Fix

Add an intermediate stop when it genuinely improves the transition. Don't keep adding colours just because you can - every stop should have a reason to be there.

2 vs 3 vs too many stops
2 colours - clean but slightly harsh
3 colours - smoother transition
6 colours - deliberately too busy

More colour stops give you more control. They also give you more opportunities to make the gradient noisy.

4. The angle changes more than the direction

This:

linear-gradient(90deg, #6c3cff, #36d1dc)

and this:

linear-gradient(135deg, #6c3cff, #36d1dc)

use exactly the same colours. But they can feel surprisingly different once they're sitting behind real content. A horizontal gradient can create a strong left-to-right movement. A diagonal gradient can feel more dynamic. A vertical gradient may work better when you want the top and bottom of a section to feel different.

Cause

You're treating the angle as a technical setting rather than part of the composition.

Fix

Choose the angle based on where the gradient will actually live - a hero background may need something different from a button, card or badge.

5. Your gradient may be competing with the content

A gradient can look beautiful on its own and fail completely once you put text on top of it. This is especially common with hero sections. One side of the background may make white text beautifully readable. A few hundred pixels away, the same text almost disappears.

Cause

You've designed the gradient as an image rather than as the background to actual content.

Fix

Test it with the real content on top. If text sits on one side of the hero, deliberately keep that part of the gradient quieter or darker.

6. The middle can become muddy

Sometimes your starting colour looks good. Your ending colour looks good. And the bit halfway between them somehow becomes the least attractive part. This is especially noticeable with certain highly saturated colours.

Cause

The browser has to calculate the colours between your stops. Depending on the colours and interpolation method, that journey can pass through a less vibrant or less visually pleasing middle.

Fix

Try adjusting one endpoint colour, the lightness, the saturation, or add an intermediate colour stop.

For more advanced work, modern CSS also provides ways to influence the colour space used for interpolation. Where appropriate and supported by your target browsers, gradients can interpolate in perceptual colour spaces such as OKLab - the chosen interpolation space can visibly change the colours produced between the stops.

background: linear-gradient(
  in oklab,
  #6c3cff,
  #36d1dc
);

This is an advanced option. You do not need it to make a good gradient. The important lesson is that if the middle looks wrong, changing the two end colours is not your only option.

Stop trying to find the perfect gradient

There isn't one CSS rule that makes every gradient look good. The same gradient can look completely different depending on the size of the element, the content sitting over it, the surrounding colours, the angle, the position of the stops, and how much attention the background is supposed to attract.

The useful habit is to stop changing everything at once. Change one thing. Look at the result. Then decide whether it solved the actual problem.

Why this matters

A gradient can be technically correct and still be wrong for the design. The browser can tell you whether your CSS is valid. It can't tell you whether your midpoint feels muddy, whether the angle is fighting the composition, or whether the background is making your headline harder to read. That's why gradients are much easier to build when you can see the result while you change the values.

You don't need to guess percentages and angles in your code editor until something finally looks right. Open the Gridlock Holmes Gradient Tool, move the colours and stops visually, and copy the CSS when you're happy with the result.
